Does a second source agree?

We compare our result with a published study that used a different record: OMI until 2014, then the European GOME-2B sensor until 2021. We compare like with like: our test on the study's own years (2005–2021) against the study. A verdict of rising or falling needs both to find a clear trend in the same direction on those years. If only one of them finds it, the sources disagree. If neither does but our longer record does, the trend is recent: we report it and mark it "one source so far". Agreement means the same direction only. The published trends are 1.1 to 5 times larger than ours, and the table shows by how much.

All trends in this table are in 10¹⁵ molecules per cm² per year. "Clear" means significant at the 5% level. The ratio is shown only where both find a clear trend in the same years. Published values: Sharma et al. (2023), Environmental Science: Atmospheres 3, 905–918, supplementary Table S2, November 2004 to March 2021. The paper does not say whether its ± is one standard error or a 95% range. We use the strict reading (a published trend is clear only when it is at least twice its ±); where the lenient reading would give a different verdict, the table says so. The published numbers are often larger than ours. The paper does not state the area it averaged over, and after 2014 it uses a different sensor, so the two are not expected to match exactly.

Test your own data

Export any daily or monthly time series from NASA Giovanni as CSV and drop it here. The same test runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.

or drop a file on this box
  1. Open giovanni.gsfc.nasa.gov, log in with a free NASA Earthdata account, and choose "Time Series, Area-Averaged".
  2. Pick a variable, a date range of ten years or more, and draw a box around your city.
  3. Plot it, then download the CSV from the Downloads tab.

One source only: with a single record we can say "rising", "falling" or "not proven", but nothing has confirmed it. For an uploaded file a day counts as usable when it has any value, so the share of usable days can be higher than in our own cell-by-cell count. The sample file is our daily Tashkent series.

How the test works

  1. Each of the 14 regions is represented by a box over its regional centre (Tashkent region by Almalyk). Three boxes over open desert (Kyzylkum west and east, Ustyurt) give the background. For each region and season we also test the difference "region minus background"; a clear rise of that difference earns the red flag.
  2. For each day we average the four grid cells (0.5° × 0.5°) around the city. A day counts only if at least two cells have a reading.
  3. Each day is compared with the normal level for its calendar month. A season's value is the season's normal level plus the average of these daily differences, so a year in which the satellite happened to see more December days than February days is not mistaken for a change in the air. A season needs at least 10 usable days.
  4. We measure the trend with the Sen slope (the middle value of the slopes between every pair of years) and test it with the Mann–Kendall test, corrected for year-to-year memory (Hamed and Rao). All 56 region-season tests (14 regions × 4 seasons) are adjusted together (Benjamini–Hochberg), so that about 5% at most of the trends we call clear are expected to be false alarms. The page shows the p-value before and after this adjustment.
  5. We work out the smallest trend this record would detect nine times out of ten (after Weatherhead et al.), from the same uncertainty as the 95% range. A smaller real change would probably be missed.
  6. We compare with the second source on the same years and give a verdict: rising or falling, sources disagree, or not proven yet. A trend that only our longer record shows is marked "one source so far".

Some rules were changed after we saw the first results, including how a verdict is formed from the two sources. Every such change, why we made it and what it did to each verdict is listed in the change log of METHOD.md in the project repository, with the code and the list of data files.

Words we use

NO₂
Nitrogen dioxide, a gas made when fuel burns: traffic, power plants, heating.
OMI
The Ozone Monitoring Instrument on NASA's Aura satellite. It has measured NO₂ once a day since 2004.
Usable reading
A day on which the satellite measured NO₂ over the city with less than 30% cloud.
Column
The satellite counts the NO₂ in the whole air column above the city, in units of 10¹⁵ molecules per cm². It is not the same as the air at street level.
Trend
The steady change per year, here shown as percent of the average level per decade.
Range
The 95% confidence interval: the values of the trend that fit the data. If the range includes zero, the direction is unknown.
Significant, or clear
If there were no real trend, a pattern this strong would appear by chance less than 5% of the time.
p-value
How often chance alone would give a pattern this strong if there were no real trend. Below 0.05 counts as clear.
±
The uncertainty a study states around its own number.
Not proven yet
The test found no clear trend. It does not mean the air is clean or stable. It means this record cannot tell.

Limits

  • Every verdict here is about the NO₂ column above the city: not the air at street level, and not PM2.5. Tashkent's worst winter pollutant is fine particles (PM2.5), which this record does not measure, and we have not compared our numbers with a ground monitor.
  • OMI passes once a day in the early afternoon and misses morning and evening peaks.
  • The satellite only reads on fairly clear days, and clear winter days may be cleaner or dirtier than cloudy ones. We have not tested this yet.
  • Usable days became fewer over the record in every season. Comparing each day with its own month limits the effect on the trend but cannot remove it.
  • The record joins two copies of the same NASA product: an open copy to May 2020 and NASA Giovanni from June 2020. On the five months they share, the same days are usable and the values differ by about 1%. That check covers only five months, and the winter rise in Tashkent depends on the years after the join: with the record cut at 2020 the same test cannot tell the winter direction. We therefore call the winter rise unconfirmed.
  • In its last years Aura no longer kept its orbit fixed, so its daily overpass drifted later in the afternoon. We have not corrected for this; a later overpass can change the NO₂ the satellite sees.
  • The published study ends in 2021. A trend that appears only later has one source so far.
  • The second source shares the OMI sensor until 2014, so it is only partly independent.
